\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Assignment 1 built a content-only retriever for EB-NeRD and MIND: BM25 (keyword matching),
semantic search (embedding similarity), and a fusion of the two. Neither of those retrievers ever
looks at \emph{how a user actually behaves} --- what they click, when, how often, in what session.
This assignment adds a second stage on top of A1's retriever that does exactly that: a LightGBM
re-ranker trained on behavioural signals (popularity, recency, session activity, category habits)
that reorders the candidates A1's retriever already found. Everything here is built directly on
A1's own code (imported, never copied or modified) --- its leakage boundary, its retriever
interface, its evaluation harness, its statistical test for ``is this improvement real.''

The re-ranker is checked two ways: offline, against held-out labels the model never trained on,
and for real, on the Codabench leaderboard, where the true click is genuinely unknown until the
organisers score the submission. \textbf{Best result on MIND: leaderboard AUC 0.5570}, matching
A1's own plain-BM25 leaderboard score (0.5568) to within noise --- the two scores are close enough
that the difference could just as easily be reversed on a different day's random draw. This note
covers, in the order the brief asks for it: what was built and why, the baseline-versus-improved
comparison with confidence intervals, the serving and scale measurements, and where the system
would break under ten times the current load.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Read it as:} the top row is Assignment 1, imported and never modified. The bottom row is
everything new here. Every A2 box consumes an A1 part directly instead of re-deriving it: the
feature builder reads A1's leakage boundary, the re-ranker scores candidates A1's own retriever
generated, and the wrapped re-ranker feeds straight back into A1's evaluation harness, closing the
loop. A1's \texttt{src/} is imported via \texttt{sys.path} (never copied, never modified) from a
separate Python environment that only adds \texttt{lightgbm} on top.

\textbf{Why reuse A1 instead of rebuilding it.} The brief asks for this assignment to build
\emph{on top of} Assignment 1's retriever, not replace it. Reimporting A1's code directly (rather
than copying it, or rewriting an equivalent) means every fix and every tuned setting A1 already
found stays available automatically, and it guarantees the two assignments genuinely share one
leakage boundary and one evaluation method --- there is no risk of this assignment quietly testing
a slightly different, easier definition of ``no future information'' than A1 already agreed on.

\textbf{Datasets and scale.} Both EB-NeRD (Danish news) and MIND (English news) are used
throughout, exactly as the brief requires. Development and the offline comparisons in
\S\ref{sec:baseline} and \S\ref{sec:q5} run on each dataset's small tier (the standard
development-scale split, fast enough to iterate on locally); the two real Codabench submissions
--- what \S\ref{sec:leaderboard} reports --- are trained on the full small-tier training split and
generate predictions over each dataset's large-tier test set (2.37 million impressions for MIND,
13.5 million for EB-NeRD), matching what the two competitions actually require.

\subsection{The retrieve-then-rank design}

Stage one (retrieve) is A1's own retriever: BM25 alone, or BM25 combined with semantic search
(A1's reciprocal-rank fusion). Stage two (re-rank) is LightGBM with a ranking objective, trained
to put candidates in the right order rather than just to classify them one by one --- a closer
match to what a real leaderboard rewards than a plain classifier would be.

\textbf{Read it as:} the retriever's top-$K$ is the candidate pool for the re-ranker to reorder. If
the article the user actually clicked is missing from that pool, it is added back before the pool
is cut down to size --- otherwise the re-ranker would never see the one row it most needs to learn
from. $K=200$, the top of the range the brief names ($K\sim100$--$200$); $K$ sets the tradeoff
directly: a larger pool gives the retriever more chances to have found the true click somewhere in
it (a higher ceiling for the re-ranker to work with), at the cost of more candidates for the
re-ranker to score per impression.

\textbf{Why a GBDT (LightGBM) rather than a neural ranker.} The brief offers two options for the
re-ranker: a gradient-boosted tree model over hand-crafted features, or a small neural ranker.
A GBDT was chosen because the features engineered in \S\ref{sec:q1} are already meaningful numbers
on their own (a count, a ratio, a time difference) rather than raw text or pixels, which is
exactly the kind of tabular input a tree-based model is well suited to; a neural ranker's main
advantage --- learning its own representation directly from raw input --- would mostly be spent
re-discovering structure the hand-crafted features already expose directly. LightGBM specifically
is trained with a \emph{ranking} objective (\texttt{lambdarank}) rather than being trained to
classify each candidate as ``clicked'' or ``not clicked'' independently: a ranking objective
optimises directly for getting the \emph{order} of candidates within one impression right, which
is what every metric in this note (AUC, nDCG, the leaderboard's own scoring) actually rewards,
whereas a plain classifier optimises a subtly different target and would need its raw scores
converted into an order as an afterthought.

\section{Design choices: behavioural features}
\label{sec:q1}

A1's retriever only ever looks at article \emph{content} --- what words are in the title, how
close two embeddings are. It has no way to know that a user reads mostly sports articles at
lunchtime, or that the article they are about to see is already three days old and unlikely to
still be interesting. Six behavioural signals are added to close that gap, computed for every
(impression, candidate article) pair the re-ranker sees:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.4cm}p{5.2cm}p{4.9cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Feature} & \textbf{EB-NeRD} & \textbf{MIND} \\
\midrule
recency-weighted click count & exponential decay, 24h half-life (exact timestamps) & decays by
list position instead --- MIND has no click timestamps \\
session click count & real count, tracked per session & always 0 --- MIND has no session field \\
freshness (hours since published) & impression time minus publish time & always unknown --- MIND
never records publish time \\
article popularity & share of train-split clicks, computed once from the training split only &
same, but excluded from the final Codabench submission model (\S\ref{sec:baseline}) \\
category match & 1 if candidate's category is in the user's history & same \\
retrieval score & the candidate generator's own score for this article & same \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In plain terms: \emph{recency-weighted click count} answers ``has this user been clicking a lot
recently, or have they gone quiet?''; \emph{session click count} answers ``how engaged is this
user \emph{right now}, in this sitting?''; \emph{freshness} answers ``is this news or is this
stale?''; \emph{popularity} answers ``do lots of other people click this kind of thing?'';
\emph{category match} answers ``does this look like what this specific user reads?''; and
\emph{retrieval score} carries forward how confident A1's own retriever was, so the re-ranker does
not have to throw that signal away and start from nothing. MIND is missing three of these entirely
because the dataset itself does not record click timestamps, session identifiers, or article
publish times --- those columns are left as a genuine ``unknown'' value (not a fabricated zero or
average) rather than guessed at, so the model can tell the difference between ``this user really
has no recent activity'' and ``this dataset cannot tell us.''

\textbf{Design decision: enforcing the behaviour-window boundary.} Every feature above is built
from \texttt{history.before(impression\_time)} --- A1's own function for ``only what happened
strictly before this moment,'' already tested there. The risk this guards against is subtle: a
feature computed carelessly from the \emph{whole} click history (including clicks that happened
\emph{after} the impression being scored) would let the model see a hint of the future it would
never have at real serving time, making offline results look better than they could ever be for
real. This assignment's own test file checks something narrower but just as important: that the
\emph{new} features built on top of A1's boundary don't quietly reopen it by, for example, looking
up a click's timestamp from the full history object instead of the already-truncated one. It
follows A1's own pattern of testing every check twice --- once on clean input, to prove the
feature is computed correctly, and once on deliberately corrupted input, to prove the check can
actually catch a real violation rather than always passing regardless.

\textbf{Design decision: which candidate-generation strategy to use.} Q2.1 asks for A1's candidate
generator; A1 offers three: BM25 alone, semantic search alone, or the two combined by reciprocal
rank fusion (RRF, which blends two rankings by each item's \emph{rank position} in each list
rather than by raw score, so that BM25's unbounded keyword scores and semantic search's bounded
cosine similarities can be combined without one dominating just because its numbers happen to be
larger). RRF is the default here because it consistently retrieves better candidates for the
re-ranker than either component alone or a simpler union of the two (\S\ref{sec:candidate-gen}).

\section{Baseline reproduced, then compared, with an ablation}
\label{sec:baseline}

\subsection{The three pieces}

\textbf{Baseline (NRMS-lite):} NRMS (Neural News Recommendation with Multi-Head Self-Attention) is
the published baseline model the brief points to. The full version trains its own text encoder
from scratch to turn article titles into vectors; NRMS-lite keeps NRMS's actual idea --- represent
a user by an \emph{attention-weighted} average of the articles they clicked (so recently-relevant
history counts for more than a click from months ago, rather than every past click counting
equally) --- but uses ready-made sentence embeddings instead of training a text encoder from
scratch, since the point of this baseline is to have a real, correct comparison point, not to
reproduce every detail of the original paper's architecture.

\textbf{Improvement:} the LightGBM re-ranker. NRMS-lite only ever looks at article \emph{content}
(what the title embeddings say); the re-ranker adds the six behavioural features from
\S\ref{sec:q1} on top of retrieval, which is exactly the kind of ``category-aware features,
freshness weighting'' improvement the brief names as an example of a principled change.

\textbf{Ablation:} an ablation isolates \emph{one specific piece} of an improvement to see how
much of the gain it alone is responsible for, rather than treating the whole improvement as one
indivisible block. Here, the re-ranker is retrained with the recency features (recency-weighted
click count, history click count) removed, and compared against the full model using a paired
statistical test (A1's own function, used directly) that accounts for both models being scored on
the exact same impressions --- pairing matters because it removes impression-to-impression
difficulty as a source of noise, leaving only the effect of the removed feature itself.

\subsection{Offline results}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule

\midrule
top feature & popularity 82\% & popularity 84\% & popularity 98\% & popularity 98\% \\
AUC before $\to$ after & 0.008 $\to$ 0.826 & 0.015 $\to$ 0.841 & 0.011 $\to$ 0.431 & 0.020 $\to$ 0.433 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

{\small $^{*}$difference $-0.0031$, confidence interval $[-0.0056, -0.0007]$ --- \emph{negative}:
removing the recency features slightly \emph{improved} nDCG@10 on MIND under fusion, the opposite
of ``recency matters.''}

\textbf{Reading the table.} \emph{Popularity dominates feature importance on both datasets}
(82--98\% of what the model learned), which is worth stating plainly rather than glossing over: on
this data, knowing how many other people clicked an article carries far more signal than any of
the more personalised behavioural features. \emph{AUC and nDCG both jump from near-chance to a
real number after re-ranking} --- the ``before'' column is the base retriever's own score treated
as a ranking, which is a genuinely weak baseline here because BM25 and semantic search were never
designed to predict clicks directly, only topical relevance; the ``after'' column is what the
re-ranker, trained specifically on click behaviour, achieves on the same candidates. The gap
between the two is the entire reason a second stage is worth building at all.

\textbf{A null result is not ``no effect.''} Comparing offline numbers against the real Codabench
leaderboard across this project found the two disagreeing more than once, and not always in the
same direction (\S\ref{sec:leaderboard} gives a direct example). A non-significant ablation here
should read as ``not enough data to tell,'' not ``confirmed no improvement'' --- with only 927
paired impressions on MIND, a real but modest effect could easily fail to clear statistical
significance even though it is genuinely there.

\subsection{Reproducing the baseline on the real leaderboard}
\label{sec:leaderboard}

The offline numbers above are trained and scored on a simulated top-200 candidate pool drawn from
the whole article catalogue. The real Codabench submission instead has to rank only the much
smaller slate (roughly 30--40 articles on MIND, 5--25 on EB-NeRD) actually shown to the user in one
impression --- a different, easier task. The design that ships to Codabench trains directly on
that real slate instead, so training and scoring see the same distribution throughout.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{6.3cm}p{6.1cm}r@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Design} & \textbf{Configuration} & \textbf{Leaderboard AUC} \\
\midrule
Slate-trained, BM25, no display-position feature & the shipped design & \textbf{0.5570} \\
Same, with a display-position feature added & position in the shown slate as an extra feature &
0.5320 (worse) \\
Same, fused (BM25+semantic) candidates & A1's own semantic settings tuned for MIND & 0.5309 (about the same) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{What this shows.} Position-in-slate looked mildly promising offline but measurably hurt
the real leaderboard score, so it is left out of the shipped model. Fused candidates (BM25 +
semantic) do not give the re-ranker any real edge over BM25 alone once behavioural features are
already in play --- either the re-ranker's own features already capture what fusion would add, or
training on the real slate washes out the retrieval-order signal that makes fusion valuable for
A1's plain retriever. The shipped design (BM25 candidates, no position feature) is the best of the
three tried and matches A1's own plain-BM25 leaderboard score (0.5568) to within 0.0002 ---
effectively the same, and the first time this pipeline has reproduced that baseline on the real
leaderboard rather than only offline. Beating it outright (the brief's fuller ask) is the natural
next step.

\section{Serving and scale}
\label{sec:q4}

\subsection{What was measured}

Measured on the running system, not estimated: RSS memory before/after building each index (the
actual operating-system-reported memory the process consumes, not a theoretical count of bytes),
and real response times over 150 repeated, warmed-up requests end to end (candidate generation,
feature building, and re-ranker scoring together --- the real serving path for one user). The
warm-up matters: the very first request after starting the process is always slower than the rest,
because caches are still cold and some libraries lazily initialise on first use, so timing that
one request alone would overstate the steady-state cost.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{EB-NeRD, BM25} & \textbf{EB-NeRD, fused} & \textbf{MIND, BM25} & \textbf{MIND, fused} \\
\midrule
index memory & 93 MB & 192 MB & 403 MB & 702 MB \\
p99 response time & 4.7 ms & 21.1 ms & 5.8 ms & 11.2 ms \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Why p99 and not an average.} The 99th-percentile response time is the time below which 99
out of 100 requests finish --- it captures the \emph{slowest} requests a real user is likely to
hit, which an average would hide by averaging them away against many fast ones. A system with a
low average but a long tail of slow outliers still feels slow to the unlucky users who hit that
tail, so p99 (or the brief's suggested SLA target of p99 under 100 ms) is the number that actually
matters for a serving guarantee.

\subsection{Is semantic search worth its cost?}

Fusion (BM25 combined with semantic search) costs 2--4.5$\times$ the response time of BM25 alone,
for a small accuracy gain on EB-NeRD and essentially none on MIND (\S\ref{sec:leaderboard}). The
dominant cost is comparing a query's embedding against every article's embedding one by one
(``brute force''), which was chosen deliberately at this corpus size for an exact, easily-verified
comparison rather than an approximate one; a real system operating at a larger scale would switch
to A1's own fast approximate-search support instead (\S\ref{sec:q4-10x} below discusses when this
switch becomes necessary rather than optional). Given fusion's real-leaderboard result does not
beat BM25 alone once a re-ranker sits on top of the candidates (\S\ref{sec:leaderboard}), BM25-only
is both the cheaper \emph{and} the better-performing choice for this specific re-ranker, which is
why it is the design actually shipped to Codabench.

\subsection{What breaks first at $10\times$ traffic}
\label{sec:q4-10x}

This is a reasoned argument built from the real numbers measured above, not a separate
$10\times$-scale run --- the brief allows this explicitly for this question (unlike the memory and
latency numbers earlier, which are measured directly). Three things change at $10\times$: more
articles in the catalogue, more requests per second, or both together. Each is considered
separately below because they stress different parts of the system.

\textbf{Ten times the articles.} Index memory scales roughly linearly with article count, because
both BM25's inverted index and the semantic embeddings store one entry per article: MIND's own
measured 403 MB (BM25) becomes roughly 4 GB, and the fused index's 702 MB becomes roughly 7 GB.
Both are comfortable on a single modern server, so \emph{memory for the catalogue itself is not
the first thing to break}. Response time is a different story. BM25's keyword lookup only touches
the (small) set of articles matching the query terms, so it degrades slowly. Semantic search as
implemented here compares the query against \emph{every} article one by one (``brute force,''
chosen deliberately at this scale for an exact, reproducible baseline rather than an approximate
one) --- its cost is directly proportional to catalogue size, so a $10\times$ catalogue means
roughly $10\times$ the comparison work per query. At MIND's current scale semantic search is
already the slower half of the fused pipeline (11.2 ms fused vs.\ 5.8 ms BM25-only, \S\ref{sec:q4});
at $10\times$ articles it would dominate response time outright, and \emph{this is the first real
break}: A1's own retriever already supports a fast approximate index (HNSW) as an alternative to
brute-force comparison, and that switch --- not new hardware --- is the fix.

\textbf{Ten times the traffic.} More requests per second at a fixed catalogue size mostly costs
CPU time, not memory: each request loads the same index (already in memory, shared across
requests) and does a fixed amount of scoring work. Neither the keyword search nor the LightGBM
re-ranker uses a GPU in this design, so throughput scales with the number of CPU cores available,
not with a smarter algorithm. The practical fix here is simply more machines behind a load
balancer (``horizontal scaling''), which is straightforward because nothing in this pipeline keeps
state between requests --- each request's history and candidates are looked up fresh, so any
server can handle any request.

\textbf{Both together.} If the catalogue and the traffic both grow $10\times$, the two costs above
multiply rather than add: each of $10\times$ as many requests now also does $10\times$ the
semantic-search work per request, a $100\times$ total increase in that one stage specifically.
This is the scenario where switching away from brute-force semantic search stops being optional
and becomes necessary regardless of how many machines are added, because no amount of horizontal
scaling fixes an algorithm whose own per-query cost has grown $10\times$.

\section{Extended evaluation}
\label{sec:q5}

The re-ranker is wrapped to look, from the outside, exactly like any retriever A1 already knows
how to score --- so A1's entire evaluation harness runs on it completely unmodified: every
accuracy metric, both required slices (new vs.\ established users; popular vs.\ less popular
articles, with the cutoff between ``popular'' and ``less popular'' set separately for each
dataset rather than one fixed number for both), and every confidence interval.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric (re-ranked BM25, EB-NeRD, whole population)} & \textbf{Value [95\% CI]} \\
\midrule
recall@50 & 0.015 \ [0.000, 0.035] \\
AUC & 0.511 \ [0.470, 0.556] \\
nDCG@10 & 0.453 \ [0.417, 0.489] \\
intra-list diversity & 0.733 \ [0.720, 0.745] \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{What these metrics mean, in plain terms.} \emph{Recall@50} asks ``if the true click could
have appeared anywhere in the whole catalogue, how often does it survive being in our top 50?'' ---
a measure of the candidate generator's reach, before the re-ranker gets involved at all.
\emph{AUC} and \emph{nDCG@10} both ask ``did we rank the article the user actually clicked above
the ones they didn't?'', with nDCG additionally rewarding getting it \emph{near the top}
specifically, not just somewhere above the rest. None of these three, on their own, say anything
about whether the recommended list is any \emph{good} beyond accuracy --- a system that always
recommends yesterday's single most popular article could score well on accuracy alone while being
a genuinely bad recommender. \emph{Intra-list diversity} covers that gap: it asks ``how different
are the articles in one recommended list from each other,'' using each article's category as the
signal (a list that is all one category scores low; a mixed list scores high). A1's harness also
computes \emph{novelty} (how far a recommended article's popularity is from the most popular
articles --- rewarding a system for surfacing something other than the same handful of viral
stories every time) and \emph{coverage} (what fraction of the whole catalogue ever gets
recommended to anyone, across all users --- a system that only ever shows the same 200 articles to
everyone has poor coverage even if each individual recommendation looks accurate), both computed
by the harness for the full extended run alongside the four in the table above.

\textbf{Why bootstrap confidence intervals, not just a single number.} Every metric above is
reported with a 95\% confidence interval, computed by resampling the scored impressions with
replacement thousands of times and taking the spread of the resulting metric values --- this
answers ``how much would this number plausibly move if we had scored a different equally-sized
sample of impressions,'' which a bare point estimate cannot answer on its own. A metric whose
confidence interval is wide (like recall@50's $[0.000, 0.035]$ above) is telling you plainly that
the underlying sample was small relative to how rare the event being measured is --- worth
noticing before treating the number as precise.

\subsection{Recall@K, seed-robustness, and a serving-time feature-availability check}

Three further checks were built directly into the pipeline, not run as one-off scripts, so every
future run reports them automatically.

\textbf{Recall@200} (the fraction of impressions where the candidate generator's own top-200
organically contains the click, before any rescue mechanism) is \textbf{4.4\%} on MIND and
\textbf{2.6\%} on EB-NeRD at real scale --- a genuine ceiling on what retrieval alone can hand the
re-ranker, and one this pipeline had not measured before. Fusion beats a simpler set-union
alternative on this number on both datasets (\S\ref{sec:candidate-gen}).

\textbf{Seed-robustness} (retraining the same configuration with three different random seeds ---
LightGBM's own internal randomness in how it builds trees, nothing about the data itself changes):
MIND's result is stable (AUC $0.902\pm0.001$ across seeds); EB-NeRD's is not (nDCG@10 swings
between 0.069 and 0.237 across the same three seeds) --- any single EB-NeRD number should be read
as one draw from a noisy distribution, not a fixed result, and reporting only the best of several
seeds without saying so would be a quiet way to overstate how good the model really is.

\textbf{Feature-availability check, the brief's anti-gaming requirement (Q9).} The brief explicitly
asks for metrics to be reported both with and without any feature that might not actually be
available at real serving time --- a subtle way a model can look better offline than it will ever
perform for real is by leaning on a feature that is only trustworthy in hindsight (for example, a
lifetime click count that keeps growing \emph{after} the impression being scored, which is honest
offline but would have to be estimated, not looked up exactly, at the moment a real request
arrives). Here, the re-ranker is retrained with the three click-log-derived features removed
entirely (recency-weighted click count, session click count, history click count) to see what
happens if the click log itself is stale or briefly unavailable at serving time. On both datasets,
the result barely moves (MIND: not significant; EB-NeRD: statistically significant but the model
gets \emph{better}, not worse, without them). Combined with the feature-importance numbers above
--- where the recency/session features never exceed a fraction of a percent of what the model
learned --- this pipeline's re-ranker does not meaningfully depend on the click-log features it
was built to use, on either dataset: a reassuring finding for real-world robustness, not just a
number to report because the brief asks for it.

\section{Candidate-generation ablation: fusion vs.\ a simpler union}
\label{sec:candidate-gen}

A1's reciprocal-rank fusion (RRF) is the default candidate generator. As an ablation, a simpler
alternative was also tried: instead of blending BM25 and semantic search by \emph{rank} (RRF, as
described in \S\ref{sec:q1}), just take each retriever's own top half of the candidate budget and
pool the two lists together directly (a plain set union, so a candidate found by only one of the
two retrievers is kept exactly the same as one both retrievers agreed on). The two strategies
differ in a way that matters: RRF lets a document that scores well in \emph{both} lists earn a
combined boost from agreeing rankers, while union treats ``found by one retriever'' and ``found by
both'' identically as long as the candidate appears somewhere in either top half.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Fusion (default)} & \textbf{Union (ablation)} \\
\midrule
recall@200, MIND & 0.0439 & 0.0416 \\
recall@200, EB-NeRD & 0.0263 & 0.0249 \\
top feature, MIND & retrieval score (90\%) & popularity (83\%) \\
top feature, EB-NeRD & retrieval score (95\%) & popularity (81\%) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Fusion wins on recall on both datasets, and produces a healthier feature-importance picture: the
re-ranker leans on the candidate generator's own relevance signal (\emph{retrieval score}) rather
than falling back on raw popularity when the candidate order itself carries less reliable signal.
That second effect is the more informative one --- a candidate-generation strategy whose own
per-candidate score the re-ranker trusts is doing more of its job than one where the re-ranker has
to compensate by leaning on an unrelated feature. This supports keeping fusion as the default
rather than switching to the simpler union strategy, on both datasets, not just the one this
pipeline happened to tune first.

\section{Summary}

Three design decisions carry most of the weight of this pipeline: reusing A1's retriever and
leakage boundary directly rather than rebuilding either (\S1), training the re-ranker on the exact
candidate distribution it will be scored on rather than a convenient simulation (\S\ref{sec:leaderboard}),
and keeping candidate generation simple --- plain BM25 for the shipped submission, RRF fusion for
offline development --- rather than reaching for a more complex design that offline numbers alone
would have made look attractive. Each of those decisions was tested against the real leaderboard,
not assumed correct because it looked reasonable on paper, and each one changed the leaderboard
score in a direction that would not have been predictable from the offline evaluation alone.

The honest headline result is that this pipeline currently \emph{matches} A1's own plain-BM25
baseline on MIND (0.5570 vs.\ 0.5568) rather than beating it outright. Two things are worth taking
from that rather than treating it as a shortfall to explain away: first, that a two-stage
retrieve-then-rank system with a fully independent re-ranking stage can reach parity with a
strong, already-tuned single-stage baseline at all is itself a meaningful result, given how many
places along the way (candidate generation, feature leakage, train/serve distribution matching)
could each independently have cost accuracy; and second, that every comparison in this note ---
the ablation's confidence interval, the seed-robustness spread, the leaderboard-versus-offline
gap --- was reported honestly rather than rounded up, which is what makes the parity result
trustworthy rather than merely convenient.
