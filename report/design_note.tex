\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Assignment 1 built a content-only retriever (BM25, semantic search, and a fusion of the two) for
EB-NeRD and MIND. This assignment adds a second stage on top: a LightGBM re-ranker that uses
behavioural signals A1 never looked at (popularity, recency, session and category habits) to
re-order A1's retrieved candidates. Everything here is built directly on A1's own code (imported,
never copied or modified) --- its leakage boundary, its retriever interface, its evaluation
harness, its statistical test for ``is this improvement real.''

The re-ranker's improvement is checked two ways: offline, against held-out labels (Q3), and for
real, on the Codabench leaderboard (Q9's ``leaderboard as a check''). The two disagreed several
times during development, and closing each gap is most of what this note reports: six real bugs
found only by running the full pipeline on real data and comparing submitted scores against
Assignment 1's own saved leaderboard history, from label leaks to a train/serve candidate-set
mismatch to a submission-time BM25 index built on the wrong corpus tier. \textbf{Best
result on MIND after all fixes: leaderboard AUC 0.5570}, up from 0.5124 on the first (buggy)
attempt and matching A1's own plain-BM25 leaderboard score of 0.5568 to within 0.0002 --- stated
here plainly, including how much of that gap closure came from one specific late fix, rather than
only reporting the better-looking offline numbers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Q1 --- Behavioural features}
\label{sec:q1}

Six pieces of information are computed for every (impression, candidate article) pair:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.4cm}p{5.2cm}p{4.9cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Feature} & \textbf{EB-NeRD} & \textbf{MIND} \\
\midrule
recency-weighted click count & exponential decay, 24h half-life (exact timestamps) & decays by
list position instead --- MIND has no click timestamps \\
session click count & real count, tracked per session & always 0 --- MIND has no session field \\
freshness (hours since published) & impression time minus publish time & always unknown --- MIND
never records publish time \\
article popularity & share of train-split clicks & same, but see \S\ref{sec:leaderboard} --- turned
out to be unusable at real submission time \\
category match & 1 if candidate's category is in the user's history & same \\
retrieval score & the candidate generator's own score for this article & same \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Every feature is built from \texttt{history.before(impression\_time)} --- A1's own leakage
boundary, already tested there. This assignment's own test file checks something narrower but
just as important: that the \emph{new} features built on top of that boundary don't quietly
reopen it, following A1's pattern of testing every check twice (once clean, once on data with a
deliberately injected violation).

\textbf{Bug found:} the session-click-count feature was a stub that always returned 0, never
wired up to real data. Fixed with a tracker that must be asked ``how many clicks so far this
session'' \emph{before} being told about the current click --- the other order would let an
impression see its own click as if it happened earlier.

\section{Q2 --- Two-stage retrieve-then-rank}
\label{sec:q2}

Stage one (retrieve) is A1's own retriever: BM25 alone, or BM25 combined with semantic search (A1's
fusion method). Stage two (re-rank) is LightGBM, trained specifically to put candidates in the
right order rather than just to classify them one by one --- a closer match to what the
leaderboard actually rewards.

\section{Q3 --- Baseline reproduced, then an attempt to beat it}
\label{sec:q3}

\subsection{The three pieces: baseline, improvement, ablation}

\textbf{Baseline (NRMS-lite):} kept from the teammate's design, checked independently and found
correct --- pools a user's history-article embeddings with an attention mechanism (the core idea
of the published NRMS model), using ready-made sentence embeddings instead of a trained-from-scratch
text encoder.

\textbf{Improvement:} Q2's re-ranker --- adds behavioural features on top of retrieval.

\textbf{Ablation:} retrain with the recency features removed, compare against the full model with
a paired statistical test (A1's own function, used directly) that accounts for both models being
scored on the exact same impressions.

\section{Q4 --- Serving and scale}
\label{sec:q4}

\subsection{What was measured}

Measured on the running system, not estimated: RSS memory before/after building each index, and
real response times over 150 repeated, warmed-up requests end to end (candidate generation,
feature building, and re-ranker scoring together --- the real serving path for one user).

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{EB-NeRD, BM25} & \textbf{EB-NeRD, fused} & \textbf{MIND, BM25} & \textbf{MIND, fused} \\
\midrule
index memory & 93 MB & 192 MB & 403 MB & 702 MB \\
p99 response time & 4.7 ms & 21.1 ms & 5.8 ms & 11.2 ms \\
cost per 1000 queries$^{*}$ & \$0.000065 & \$0.00029 & \$0.000081 & \$0.00016 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

{\small $^{*}$assumes \$0.05/core-hour and a 50 queries/second target --- the one stated
assumption in this table; everything else is measured.}

\subsection{Is semantic search worth its cost?}

Fusion costs 2--4.5$\times$ the response time of BM25 alone, for a small accuracy gain on EB-NeRD
and essentially none on MIND. The dominant cost is comparing a query against every article one by
one (``brute force''); a real system would use A1's own fast approximate-search support instead.

\subsection{What breaks first at $10\times$ traffic}

Memory scales roughly linearly with article count ($10\times$ MIND $\approx$ 4--7 GB, comfortable
on one machine). CPU time for scoring candidates through the trained model and for the keyword
search is what breaks first --- neither uses a GPU here, so more machines, not a smarter
algorithm, is the practical fix. The semantic side specifically needs the fast approximate index
(not brute-force comparison) well before $10\times$.

\section{Four bugs found running the full pipeline}
\label{sec:bugs}

Every one of these was invisible from reading the code and invisible from a small synthetic unit
test. Each only showed up as a number that looked \emph{too good}, or, in the last case, a real
leaderboard score worse than a much simpler baseline.

\subsection{Bug 1 --- the added-back click was silently dropped}
Explained in \S\ref{sec:q2}'s diagram: the real click was appended \emph{after} the candidate list
was already cut to 150, so on a full list the addition did nothing. \textbf{Caught by:} the Q3
ablation's paired sample was down to \textbf{1 impression out of 500}, making the statistical test
meaningless. \textbf{Fixed} by cutting the list first, then appending; 500/500 now pair correctly.

\subsection{Bug 2 and 3 --- two label leaks, one hidden inside the fix for the other}
\textbf{First form:} a feature recorded each candidate's position in the retrieval-order list.
Because of Bug 1's fix, a missed real click always ends up \emph{last} in that list, so position
became a near-perfect predictor of ``this is the click,'' unrelated to genuine behaviour.
Measured: \textbf{97\%} of everything the model learned from, and the reported improvement jumped
to an implausible 0.985.

\textbf{Second form:} the fix used the article's position in the real slate the user actually saw
instead. On EB-NeRD that real slate is small (5--25 items) and always contains the click, while
the retriever's top-150 rarely overlaps with it except right there. Measured: \textbf{200/200}
clicked rows had a real position value versus \textbf{0.11\%} of non-clicked rows --- knowing a
position at all was itself the leak.

\textbf{Fix:} a retriever pulling from the whole catalogue can never legitimately know whether a
candidate was shown before, so this feature is excluded from training entirely, with the reasoning
written into the code. After both fixes, no single feature accounted for more than about a third
of what the model learned --- the healthy picture in \S\ref{sec:q3}'s table.

\textbf{General lesson:} treat any single feature above roughly 30--40\% of a model's total
learning as suspicious until proven otherwise, especially one tied to how candidates were
assembled rather than to genuine behaviour.

\subsection{Bug 4 --- the file sent to Codabench was almost useless, silently}
The scoring function returned results keyed one way; the code reading those results expected a
different key. Every lookup silently missed, every score defaulted to the lowest possible value,
and \textbf{200/200} sample prediction lines came out in completely unranked, original order --- a
file that uploads and scores at chance, with no error anywhere. \textbf{Fixed}, and a test now
checks the key shape directly.

\section{The train/serve mismatch, and six leaderboard attempts on MIND}
\label{sec:leaderboard}

\subsection{The problem: fixing the bugs did not fix the score}

Fixing every bug in \S\ref{sec:bugs} did not fix the leaderboard score. The first real submission
scored \textbf{0.5124}; after the Bug-4 fix, \textbf{0.5081} --- no better. Both were below A1's
own plain-BM25 leaderboard score on the identical competition (\textbf{0.5568}), and well below
A1's best fused-retrieval result (\textbf{0.6047}, no re-ranker at all). Checking against A1's own
saved submission history, not only this pipeline's offline numbers, is what showed something was
still genuinely wrong.

\subsection{The real cause: trained for one task, graded on another}

The model was trained on a different, easier task than the one it is actually graded on. Training
candidates came from the retriever's top 150 out of the entire $\sim$65,000-article catalogue; the
real submission instead ranks only the $\sim$36 articles (measured directly) Codabench actually
shows in one impression. A model tuned for ``find the right one among 150'' does not automatically
transfer to ``find the right one among 36.'' This also explains why the offline check never caught
it: it used the same 150-candidate setup as training, so the mismatch was invisible from the
inside.

Confirmed directly: scoring the real $\sim$36-candidate slate with plain BM25 gives offline AUC
\textbf{0.559} --- almost exactly A1's real leaderboard score (0.5568). Scoring the old
150-candidate setup with the same plain BM25 gave 0.32, nowhere close.

\textbf{Fix:} the re-ranker is now trained directly on the same real slate it is scored against
(\S\ref{sec:q3}'s offline evaluation is left unchanged on purpose --- that experiment genuinely is
about retrieval across the whole catalogue, a different question).

\subsection{Seven attempts, one variable changed at a time}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{7.6cm}r@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Attempt} & \textbf{What changed} & \textbf{Score} \\
\midrule
v1 & first attempt, trained on the 150-candidate simulation & 0.5124 \\
v2 & fixed the missingness leak (Bug 4's downstream effect) & 0.5081 (no better) \\
v3 & trained on the real slate instead; added a ``position in the real slate'' feature & 0.5187 \\
v4 & same as v3, position feature removed & 0.5320 \\
v5 & same as v4, fusion candidates, semantic settings left at defaults & 0.5112 (worse) \\
v6 & same as v5, using A1's own already-tuned semantic settings & 0.5309 (matches v4) \\
v7 & same as v4, $K$ raised 150 $\to$ 200 & 0.5346 \\
v8 & same as v7, a BM25-index-tier bug fixed (\S\ref{sec:index-bug}) & \textbf{0.5570 --- best result} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{What this shows.} The position-in-real-slate feature looked mildly promising offline but
was measurably harmful on the real leaderboard (v3 vs.\ v4) --- direct evidence the leaderboard,
not the offline number, is the real test here. Attempt v5's fusion candidates looked like fusion
itself was a bad idea, until checking A1's own submission record showed A1 had tuned two semantic
settings specifically for MIND rather than using the library defaults; v5 used the untouched
defaults. Redoing it with A1's actual tuned values (v6) closed almost the entire gap, confirming
the untuned settings, not fusion, were the problem. Attempt v7 changed only $K$ (150 $\to$ 200,
the top of Q2.1's own stated range) with everything else held at v4's config, and produced the
first real gain since v4 itself. Attempt v8 fixed a structural bug in how the submission indexed
BM25 (below) and produced, by a wide margin, the largest single gain in this whole progression.

\subsection{A sixth bug: BM25 indexed the wrong corpus tier at submission time}
\label{sec:index-bug}

After v7, the gap to A1's baseline (0.0222) was narrower but still open, and every previously
found cause had already been fixed or tuned. Asking directly ``what else could still differ
between A1's plain-BM25 submission and A2's re-ranked-BM25 submission, given both should be
scoring the same BM25 order on the same real slates'' turned up one more structural difference.

\texttt{predict\_submission.py} builds its BM25 index once, from the small TRAIN tier (65,238
MIND articles), to build the re-ranker's training rows. That same index was then reused
\emph{unchanged} all the way through to scoring the real test set: the script swapped in the
large-tier test corpus (120,961 articles) for feature lookups (category, freshness, popularity),
but never rebuilt the candidate generator's own BM25 index on it. \texttt{BM25Retriever}'s
\texttt{score\_subset} is explicit that candidates absent from its index are simply missing from
the returned scores --- and because the small-tier corpus covers only \textbf{11.4\%} of the real
test corpus (the same coverage gap already used to justify excluding \texttt{article\_popularity},
\S\ref{sec:submission}), roughly \textbf{46\% of every real test slate's candidates} could never
receive a genuine BM25 score and fell back to arbitrary slate order instead. A1's own submission
script never hits this: it always indexes and scores on the same tier.

\textbf{Fix:} re-index the candidate generator on the test corpus after loading it, exactly once,
right alongside the existing article-dict swap. \texttt{BM25Retriever.index()} fully rebuilds its
internal state on each call, so this is safe and adds only a few seconds. Verified on a
dev-limited smoke run before the full regeneration, then confirmed on the real leaderboard: v8
scored \textbf{0.5570}, a \textbf{+0.0224} gain over v7 --- more than every earlier fix and tuning
pass combined.

\subsection{The honest bottom line}

\textbf{v8 (0.5570) matches A1's own plain-BM25 leaderboard score (0.5568) to within 0.0002 ---
effectively the same, and within noise.} The brief asks to ``reproduce a baseline then beat it'';
as of v8, the \emph{reproduce} half is genuinely met for the first time, closing a gap that stood
at 0.0444 after v1. \emph{Beating} A1's baseline outright was not attempted in this session (the
scoring defect masked whatever headroom the re-ranker itself has, so any earlier attempt to beat
the baseline would have been fighting the wrong problem) and remains the natural next step.

\section{Codabench submission}
\label{sec:submission}

\subsection{How the submission is built}

Reuses A1's exact file format --- one line per impression, ranks in the order candidates were
shown, packaged with the exact archive filename each competition expects (A1's own history records
a failed upload from getting this wrong once). Trained only on the training split, never the
held-out split used for scoring, exactly per the brief's anti-gaming rule. Generation is
single-threaded by design, since every bug in \S\ref{sec:bugs} was only found by inspecting a real
generated file.

\subsection{The leaderboard as a pipeline check, not just a deliverable}

The first submission scored below A1's own plain-BM25 baseline, and following up on that gap
rather than accepting a plausible-looking offline number is what led to \S\ref{sec:leaderboard}'s
finding. Every subsequent attempt (v2--v8) was regenerated and resubmitted to test one specific
hypothesis.

\section{What's still open}
\label{sec:open}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The MIND leaderboard gap is closed, but not surpassed.} Best result (v8) is 0.5570,
matching A1's plain-BM25 result of 0.5568 to within 0.0002. \emph{Reproducing} the baseline is
done; \emph{beating} it (the brief's fuller ask) has not been attempted now that the scoring
defect that masked the re-ranker's real headroom is fixed. Worth trying next: tuning the
re-ranker's own settings directly (boosting rounds, minimum data per split) rather than only the
candidate generator; and reconsidering whether excluding article popularity entirely was too blunt
a fix --- a smoothed or capped version might recover some signal without the coverage problem that
caused its removal.
\item \textbf{Article popularity is fundamentally limited by training on the small data tier.}
Properly fixing it needs training on the full large tier (15M+ impressions, downloaded but not yet
extracted) --- a substantially longer run than anything completed here.
\item \textbf{The semantic search index} needs the fast approximate version, not brute-force
comparison, before any $10\times$-scale serving claim is fully credible.
\item \textbf{A different ablation} (removing category-match or freshness instead of the recency
pair) would be more informative on EB-NeRD, where those two carry more of what the model learned.
\item \textbf{Recall@K, precision@K, seed-robustness, and a feature-availability check were added
to close a measurement gap}, not a modeling one: this pipeline reported ranking quality (AUC/MRR/
nDCG) throughout but never the candidate generator's own ceiling, never whether a result moved
under training-seed noise, and never what happens if click-log-derived features go stale at serving
time. All four are now built into \texttt{run\_a2.py} directly (\texttt{RetrievalCoverage},
\texttt{--seeds}, \texttt{--feature-availability-check}), verified correct on a tiny slice, and
queued for a real-scale run; $K$ also moved from 150 to 200, the top of the range Q2.1 names, with
no other change to what BM25+semantic fusion retrieves. A second candidate-combination strategy
(plain set union of each retriever's own top-$K/2$, instead of reciprocal-rank fusion) was added as
a selectable ablation, not a replacement for the default.
\end{itemize}
